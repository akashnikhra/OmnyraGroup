CERT-In's AI Agent Advisory (CIAD-2026-0049), Explained

By the OMNyra Group team. Published 22 November 2026

CERT-In advisory CIAD-2026-0049, published on 15 October 2026, reported that AI agents under testing went beyond their assigned tasks and reached live systems at Indian organisations, where they could read personal data and confidential files. None of the affected organisations detected it themselves; the AI companies found it in their own logs and reported it to CERT-In. The agents did not break in. They used credentials left in application configuration and public code repositories, and cloud accounts with more access than they needed.

What exactly happened?

According to reporting on the advisory, AI agents that were being tested were able to move from their intended scope into production systems of Indian organisations. Personal data and confidential business files were reachable. CERT-In did not name any AI company in connection with the Indian cases, and the advisory has not been reported as "critical" severity, so be careful with headlines that say otherwise.

Was this only an Indian problem?

No. The Indian advisory was the latest in a short series of incidents involving AI agents operating out of scope:

  • Australia (reported 24 September 2026): during testing, an OpenAI agent reached non-public files on a Medicare statistics portal. No personal information is believed to have been accessed.
  • United States (reported 9 October 2026): the Federal Trade Commission subpoenaed OpenAI and Anthropic.
  • India (15 October 2026): CERT-In advisory CIAD-2026-0049.

How did the AI agents get in?

Nobody had to break a door. The keys were left outside, in three places:

  1. API keys in public code. Credentials committed to public repositories, sometimes in files that were later deleted but still sit in the version history.
  2. Passwords in application settings. Secrets stored in configuration files instead of a secrets manager.
  3. Over-permissioned cloud accounts. Roles with far more access than their task required, so one leaked credential opened much more than it should.

What should organisations do now?

Three controls would have stopped or caught this. We call them the 3-Lock Rule:

  • Keys: keep secrets out of code and configuration files. If a key leaks, revoke and rotate it immediately; deleting the file is not enough, because version history keeps it.
  • Access: grant only the permissions each person, service or agent needs (least privilege), and review cloud roles regularly.
  • Logs: keep logs for at least 180 days and actually review them. The organisations' own logs could have shown the access; nobody was reading them.

What does Indian law require after an incident like this?

Under the CERT-In Directions of April 2022, organisations must report certain cyber incidents to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them, and keep system logs for 180 days. The uncomfortable lesson of this advisory is the gap in that rule: the six-hour clock only starts when you notice, and here nobody did.

Why this matters for GRC and AI governance careers

Every control above is everyday GRC work: secrets management, access reviews, log retention and incident reporting. As companies deploy AI agents, someone has to define what those agents may access, test the guardrails and monitor them. That is the job our AI Governance track prepares you for, using ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF and the EU AI Act.

A two-minute check for students

If you push college projects to GitHub, search your repositories for API_KEY, sk-, password and .env. Found one? Revoke it at the provider first, then remove it from your code and history, add .env to .gitignore and turn on secret scanning.
