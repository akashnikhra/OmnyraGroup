4 Cyber Incidents in One Week, 2 in India: What They Teach GRC Teams

By the OMNyra Group team. Published 22 November 2026

In one week of October 2026, four security incidents made the news, two of them involving Indian organisations: weak certificate-authority keys at eMudhra, a data-leak claim involving State Bank of India, two critical PHP vulnerabilities known as PHP2Shell, and stolen UpdraftCentral keys used to backdoor WordPress sites. They look different, but they share one pattern: weak keys, stolen keys, unpatched software and an unverified claim. Each maps to a specific ISO 27001 control.

Case 1: eMudhra's certificate authority keys

Eight digital keys belonging to eMudhra, an Indian certificate authority, were found to be weak enough to be derived. Google's researchers put it plainly: "the keys did not have to be stolen." eMudhra says its infrastructure was not compromised and that only one of the eight affected intermediates was deployed in production. CERT-In's advisory of 21 October (CIAD-2026-0050) reported no confirmed malicious use at that time.

Case 2: the State Bank of India data leak claim

SBI acknowledged a data incident after a set of roughly 375,000 records appeared on the dark web. Its own filing says 3,897 records containing personal information were identified within that set, and describes the count as preliminary. Whether the data is authentic has not been established, which is why the larger number should not be repeated as fact.

Case 3: PHP2Shell

Two PHP vulnerabilities, rated 9.2 in severity, could let an attacker take over a web server. Qualys reported that around 71% of PHP installations it observes run end-of-life versions that no longer receive security fixes. That figure is the vendor's own data, and it describes usage, not the share proven vulnerable.

Case 4: stolen UpdraftCentral keys

Attackers used stolen UpdraftCentral keys to plant backdoors in WordPress sites running the UpdraftPlus plugin. The plugin has more than four million installs; that is its install base, not the number of affected sites.

What is the common pattern?

Strip away the headlines and the same failures repeat: keys that were weak, keys that were stolen, software that was not patched, and a claim that spread faster than it could be verified. We summarise the response as three habits:

  • Patch: update PHP, WordPress and plugins promptly, and retire end-of-life versions.
  • Rotate: when a vendor reports an incident, change the keys, tokens and API keys you hold with them straight away.
  • Verify: act on confirmed facts from at least two sources, not on a single claim.

Which ISO 27001 controls apply?

HabitISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A control
Patch8.8 Management of technical vulnerabilities
Rotate (weak or stolen keys)8.24 Use of cryptography
Rotate after a vendor incident5.19 to 5.22 Supplier relationships and monitoring of supplier services
Verify before acting5.24 to 5.26 Incident management planning, assessment and response
